Their Voice First.

Andrew Hahm · April 2026 · 3 minute read

The most common executive briefing agenda in the world opens with a welcome from the host, moves into a company overview, and reaches the customer's actual problem sometime after lunch. I build mine in the opposite order.

The first working session of the day belongs to the customer. I name it for them, "Voice of" and their company name, and I protect at least thirty minutes for it. Forty-five to sixty is better. They talk. We listen. Nothing from our side gets presented before it.

Why it goes first

Because it re-tunes everything after it. Every speaker who follows has just heard, in the customer's own words, what matters this quarter. The good ones adjust on the spot. The session that was going to be a product tour becomes an answer to something the customer said an hour ago.

The executive welcome comes after their voice for the same reason. An executive who walks in and responds to what was just said is having a conversation. An executive who opens the day from a script is giving a speech. And the welcome is always assigned to a named executive. "Executive to be confirmed" on an agenda tells the customer how the day was planned.

The rules I don't trade away

Forty-five minutes or it isn't a discussion. Account teams always want to fit in one more topic. Six thirty-minute sessions is a day of status updates. Three real conversations is a day people remember.

Lunch is relationship time. No slides, an executive host, a full hour when I can get it.

Demos go late. Context first, proof second. A demo shown before the customer has said what they need is inventory. The same demo after three good discussions is an answer.

The day ends in decisions, not applause. The closing roundtable turns the feeling in the room into commitments with owners and dates. If the customer leaves impressed and nothing is agreed, the day was hospitality.

The venue is the whole visit

One last rule. The briefing does not start when the first slide goes up. It starts when the customer lands and ends when they leave: the car at the curb, badges cleared before arrival, the dinner. The agenda is the middle of that day, and customers judge all of it.

We don't present. We listen. Everything about how the day is built follows from that.

Always forward
