The Agent Is Never the Last Step.

Andrew Hahm · June 2026 · 3 minute read

There is one rule I will not bend when AI touches an executive briefing. The agent is never the last step before the executive.

The reason is simple. A briefing runs on trust, and trust fails unevenly. A hundred accurate bios earn you nothing anyone will remember. One invented quote in front of a customer's CEO ends the program's credibility, and probably the experiment with it. So every agent output passes through a named person who owns the fact-check before anything is customer-facing.

The gate is on facts, not prose

This is where most teams get the gate wrong. They picture a senior person rewriting every draft, which rebuilds the bottleneck the system was meant to remove. The reviewer is not there to improve the writing. They are certifying claims. This title is current. This number is real. This quote was said, by this person, in this place.

Checking is far faster than creating. That is what keeps the gate cheap.

Review has to scale too

If a person checks everything with the same intensity, throughput stops climbing and you are back where you started. Review has to be calibrated, never uniform. Three moves do most of the work.

Tier by risk. Anything an executive or a customer will see gets human certification, always. Internal working drafts get a spot check. Attention goes to the high-stakes surface and nowhere else.

Gate by stage. One gate at the end is too late and too heavy. A briefing has five natural checkpoints, each with an owner and one question.

Route by confidence. A well-built system flags its own unverified claims and shows its sources, so the reviewer spends time on the uncertain fraction instead of rereading the whole pack. One caveat keeps this safe. A model's opinion of its own accuracy can't be what protects a customer. Confidence routing directs attention in the lower tiers. It never skips the floor.

The reviewer is the training signal

Here is what turns review from a cost into an advantage. Every correction goes back into the system, not just into that one document. The same mistake does not get made twice, the correction load falls over time, and the judgment your best people apply at the gate becomes part of how the system works.

It also answers the question every team is quietly asking when they hear the word agents. Nobody is being automated out of the room. Their names are on the certifications. Their judgment is the standard.

The agent does the reading. A person signs.

Always forward
