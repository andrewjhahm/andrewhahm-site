Your Worst Briefing Should Be as Good as Your Best.

Andrew Hahm · July 2026 · 4 minute read

Every briefing program I have run rationed its best work. Nobody wrote that down as policy. It was just arithmetic.

A briefing built for one executive takes real reading. Four quarters of earnings calls. What that executive has said in public this year. The account history, including the parts the account team would rather skip. Then someone has to turn all of it into a bio that says something, an agenda with a point of view, and a narrative that fits this customer and no other. Done properly, that was most of an operator's day.

So we chose. Ten briefings a quarter got the full treatment, and the rest got the template. Everyone in this function knows the moment a customer can tell which one they got.

Why the tradeoff existed

Deep for a few or wide for many is the oldest tradeoff in executive engagement, and it was never about talent or effort. Bespoke synthesis was expensive human labor, and there are only so many operator days in a quarter. The template was a budget decision.

What actually got cheaper

Agentic AI changes one line in that budget. It collapses the cost of synthesis. A well-built system reads the sources and hands back a draft that is most of the way there in minutes, with its sources attached. It does not make the judgment calls, and I would not want it to. The risk call, the read on the relationship, the decision about what not to say in the room: that is still the operator, and now it is where all of the operator's time goes.

The precise version matters, because a sharp CFO will ask for it. AI doesn't make briefings cheaper. It makes a bespoke briefing cost about what a generic one used to. When built-for-this-executive costs the same as off-the-template, you stop choosing.

You stop choosing between personal and scalable. The floor of every briefing rises to your best operator's best day, and then it keeps climbing.

Three legs, or it is a slogan

"Scale without losing quality" is a slogan until three things hold it up. Take any one away and it falls over.

The second leg has one rule, and it is the only non-negotiable I have: the agent is never the last step before the executive. A wrong title or an invented quote in front of a customer's CEO ends the experiment, and it should. The gate is on facts, not prose. The reviewer is not rewriting the draft. They are certifying that this title, this number, and this quote are real. Checking is much faster than creating, which is why the gate stays cheap.

The third leg keeps everyone honest, me included. Count only the briefings that clear the rubric. You can't raise that number by shipping more junk, because junk doesn't clear. You can't raise it by polishing one showpiece, because volume counts. To get a baseline, hand-grade last quarter's briefings against the rubric before you change anything. That grade is your before picture.

The floor keeps climbing

Here is the part that compounds. Every correction a reviewer makes goes back into the system, not just into that one document. The next draft starts from a better place. The standard your strongest engagement manager set on their best day becomes the default, and then the default moves.

That changes what a briefing team is for. Less assembling, more deciding. Agents handle what's repeatable. We prepare the people who do.

If you run a program and want to test this without betting the quarter, pick one workflow. Executive research or the post-briefing recap are the usual candidates. Time it. Build that one piece with your operators in the loop, keep a person on the gate, and time it again. One skill at a time, in production.

The tradeoff was real for as long as I have done this work. It isn't anymore.

Always forward
